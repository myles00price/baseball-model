THE ENGINE

Usage first: how many plays a team runs and who gets the targets and carries, blended from 2025 per-game rates and re-mapped to 2026 rosters. Efficiency is shrunk toward position averages, adjusted for the opposing defense at half weight, and every player's numbers are forced to add up to their team's totals. Projections are then recalibrated against a full season of walk-forward testing. Click any game for the Gameday view: season stats and how often each player beat the current projection last year.

  • Rookies have no NFL data and are not projected yet (82 unsigned/retired players dropped in re-mapping).
  • Week 1 runs on 2025 data — expect the widest error bars of the year.
  • Passing-yard props are permanently out: four separate tests said the market knows better.

2026-08-06THE OPENING-LINE TEST: THE MARKET DRIFTS TOWARD OUR NUMBER

A subscriber-grade question from Myles: if the picks break even against the final line, they should be ahead of the early line - so test it. The same four weeks of prop lines were pulled again, this time as they stood three days before kickoff, and the same picks were graded against both. He was right about the direction: identical picks score about a point better against the early line than the late one. The finding that matters more, though, is which way the lines MOVED. When a receptions line changed between Tuesday and kickoff, it moved toward our projection 21 times out of 27 - and rushing lines followed us 58% of the time. In this business that is the tell: the market spending all week correcting toward your number means your number was ahead of it. Passing yards showed the mirror image - the market moved away from us, its final verdict on that market, which is now dropped for good. The plan is unchanged in discipline and sharpened in focus: a full paper season on receptions, receiving yards and rushing yards, with picks made early in the week when the value exists, every one logged and graded, no money until half a season proves it.

2026-08-06WORKHORSE-BACK FIX + FIRST TEST AGAINST REAL PROP LINES

Two updates in one day. First, the running back lowball was diagnosed and fixed: the engine was splitting carries across four backs even in weeks when two of them were in street clothes. Inactive lists are public before kickoff, so the projector now removes ruled-out players and hands their work to whoever remains - the average error on workhorse backs dropped from 15 yards to essentially zero. Second, the real test: projections for four Sunday slates of last season were compared against the actual prop lines posted two minutes before kickoff, nearly a thousand player lines across receptions, receiving yards, rushing yards and passing yards. The verdict is a perfect coin flip: 490 wins, 446 losses, 52.35% where 52.38% is breakeven after the book's cut. Inside that flat number there is one live lead - receptions picks hit 53.8%, and the engine's bigger disagreements with the line won 58% of the time, which is the shape a real edge takes - and one confirmed casualty: passing-yard picks lost outright and are dropped from consideration. Next step is the same discipline the baseball model earned its record with: a full paper season on receiving props only, every pick logged and graded in public, not a dollar at stake until the numbers prove out.

2026-08-06PIVOT: PLAYER PROPS ARE NOW THE PRODUCT

The football project has a new primary target: player props - receptions, receiving yards, rushing yards - instead of game spreads. The reasoning is the record itself: four seasons of honest testing showed the point-spread market gives up nothing, while props are a famously softer market with hundreds of lines a week and books that limit winners, which tells you who usually wins there. The engine is a usage-first projector built by hand: how many plays a team runs, who gets the targets and carries, everyone's efficiency pulled toward position averages, adjusted for the defense across the field, with every player's numbers forced to add up to the team's totals. It was immediately put through the same discipline as everything else - project every week of last season using only what was knowable at the time, then compare against what actually happened. First honest results: the engine beats a simple per-game average on receptions and receiving yards, loses slightly on rushing and passing yards, and has a known flaw to fix - it lowballs workhorse running backs by about 15 yards a game. Nothing is bet, nothing is texted; next milestone is testing projections against real posted prop lines.

2026-08-01THE SPREAD IS NOW OFFICIALLY THE PRODUCT

Clarified the football model's job description: it is a point-spread model. It builds its own number for every game, compares that to the posted spread, and speaks in the spread market's language - how likely each side is to cover, how likely the game lands exactly on the number, and the edge after the book's cut. Building this the honest way immediately taught two lessons. One: games really do land on the famous numbers - at spreads of 3 or 7 about one game in ten pushes, and the first version of the math expected a third of that; it now carries a per-number push table learned from a decade of history. Two, and more important: when the model's cover confidence was tested against reality, its boldest opinions were worthless - so a calibration layer now converts raw disagreement into how often disagreements of that size have actually won. The calibrated answer is humbling and correct: roughly 48 to 50 percent on essentially every game, which is the model telling us it has no spread edge yet. A model that admits that is worth more than one that lies, because the same machinery will report honestly the day something real shows up. Win-odds numbers still exist as a byproduct, but the spread is the deliverable.

2026-08-01FEATURE REBUILD + MODEL TOURNAMENT: LINEAR WINS, MARKET STILL UNDEFEATED

The football model was rebuilt with a wider set of ingredients - drive efficiency, success rate, turnover tendencies, scoring form, and for the first time quarterback continuity, which proved the old football adage: teams starting a new quarterback lose by 1.5 on average while stable ones win by 2.2. Every ingredient then had to earn its spot on unseen games, and most did not: turnovers and scoring form add nothing once efficiency is known, and the final model keeps just three team signals. Four different types of prediction engine were also raced head to head on four unseen seasons; the simplest one won, and the fancier tree-based engines lost outright - same lesson the baseball model taught. Bottom line: our standalone rating improved from 9.94 to 9.85 points of average error, the closing line sits at 9.49, and nothing we have built adds information the market lacks. No edge is claimed. One tree model did post a flashy 53.7% against the spread; it is flagged in the report as almost certainly luck, and will be re-tested rather than believed.
